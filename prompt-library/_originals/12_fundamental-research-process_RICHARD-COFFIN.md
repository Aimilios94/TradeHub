# Fundamental stock research process — Richard Coffin (owner-supplied, verbatim)
Source: owner. Usage in plan: the QUALITATIVE steps (1, 2, parts of 4, and 6) are the basis of the Fundamentals Lab deep-dive mode (§37). The QUANTITATIVE steps (3, 4 capital-efficiency, 5) are a candidate replacement for E5's fixed methodology (§36) — owner deferred that decision.

Step 1: Screening for Ideas
The goal of this initial step is to filter down the massive universe of stocks so you do not waste weeks doing deep-dive research on a company you ultimately do not care for.
Method: Use a stock screener to filter companies based on broad quantitative criteria, specifically looking for revenue growth, overall profitability, and debt kept within a reasonable range.
Quick Assessment: Pass the filtered tickers through a tool that summarizes key financial data (past financial performance, debt, sales, and expected consensus estimates) onto a one-page summary to get a quick overview of the company's general health.

Step 2: Understanding the Business
This is a highly qualitative phase where you adopt the mindset of a business owner trying to understand the ins and outs of a company as if you were buying a private practice.
What to analyze: Study the company's business segments, how they earn money, the nature of their revenues (contractual, recurring, or seasonal), and the geography of their operations. You must also map out key players who affect performance: customers, suppliers, employees, regulators, and management.
Industry specifics: Identify sector-specific Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to compare the business against its competitors.
Research documents: Read regulatory filings such as the annual report (10K in the US), the most recent quarterly reports, and investor presentations.
Organization: Take notes using the SWOT framework (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) and keep a "parking lot" of questions and risks to investigate later.
Goal: The primary objective here is to find Quality Companies that possess a strong competitive advantage (a "moat") and an attractive business model.

Step 3: Understanding the Finances
This is a number-heavy phase focused on analyzing the three major financial statements: the Income Statement (accounting earnings), the Balance Sheet (assets and liabilities), and the Cash Flow Statement (actual cash movements).
Key calculations:
CAGR: Compute cumulative average growth rates for revenues and profits over 5, 10, or 15-year periods to understand long-term growth averages.
Common size financial statements: Turn financial statement line items into percentages (as a percentage of revenue for the income statement, and a percentage of total assets for the balance sheet) to easily track which relative costs are growing or shrinking year-over-year.
Ratios and margins: Calculate margins and ratios focused on profitability, solvency, and liquidity to map out the company's operational strength.
Peer analysis: Compare the company's financial details and KPIs side-by-side with its competitors. Richard notes that he has sometimes pivoted to entirely new stock ideas during this stage because a competitor's numbers looked much more attractive.

Step 4: Understanding the Strategy
This step shifts from a historical review to evaluating the company's future opportunity.
Key elements: Review management's strategic priorities, planned capital expenditures (CapEx), and how they plan to finance these future plans (e.g., through more debt, issuing new shares, or hybrid instruments).
Execution track record: Evaluate whether management has a history of successfully doing what they say they will do. For instance, if they grow via acquisitions (a serial acquirer), verify that they actually optimize the businesses they buy rather than just spending investor cash to inflate growth figures.
Capital efficiency metrics: Check 5, 10, or 15-year averages of return metrics like ROCE (Return on Capital Employed), ROIC (Return on Invested Capital), and ROE (Return on Equity).
Information sources: Read management's high-level objectives in the 10K, investor presentations, and transcripts from earnings calls and conferences where management is directly questioned about future challenges.

Step 5: Valuation
Valuation is purposefully left for later in the process to prevent the stock's current price (cheap or expensive) from biasing your qualitative analysis of the company's quality.
Relative Valuation: Compare multiples like Price-to-Earnings (P/E), Enterprise Value to EBITDA (EV/EBITDA), and free cash flow yield against the company's historical averages and its peers.
Absolute Valuation: Utilize a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis to forecast future free cash flows and discount them back to today's price. This allows you to model different circumstances by changing key variables.
Entry timing: Look at qualitative indicators like insider/management buying and recent short-term news (lawsuits, headlines) to optimize your entry point.

Step 6: Review and Finalize Notes
Once you decide whether to buy immediately or wait, compile and organize your notes for long-term reference.
Gaps: Resolve the outstanding questions parked during your initial research.
Skepticism: Actively seek out conflicting reports and opposing viewpoints to challenge your thesis and sanity-check your assumptions.
Formatting: Organize notes into easily digestible bullet points and brief summaries.

## The Most Important Considerations to Keep in Mind
Operate within your Circle of Competence: You must have familiarity with the industry in which the business operates. If you do not understand the industry, avoid researching the stock until you gain that background.
Conduct Forensic Analysis & Watch for Red Flags: Never trust reported financial figures blindly. Check for related party transactions, management conflicts of interest, and accounting shenanigans. Be particularly cautious of "adjusted" financial figures (like adjusted earnings) promoted by management, and carefully inspect what they are adding back or removing, as these figures bypass standard accounting regulations. Ensure you understand exactly how any third-party data you use is aggregated or adjusted.
Evaluate the Feasibility of Corporate Plans: Many companies fail by expanding into areas where they have no experience. Look at multi-year return metrics (ROIC, ROCE, ROE) to see if management's past execution matches their promises, rather than accepting strategic presentations at face value.
Do Not Overpay (Manage Capital Impairment Risk): High-quality businesses with strong profitability, moats, and excellent management are often widely recognized and bid up to expensive valuations. Paying too much increases your risk of capital impairment. If an excellent company is too expensive, do not buy it; instead, place it on a watch list and wait for a market dip or a material event to create an attractive entry price.
Sanity Check with Opposing Views: Do not fall in love with your own investment thesis. Seek out counterarguments ("playing devil's advocate") to ensure your assumptions hold weight.
Use Notes to Ground Yourself During Market Turmoil: Having organized, written notes of your original fundamental thesis is vital. When a stock price plunges and the market panics, you can refer to your notes to determine if the fundamental business has actually changed or if the panic is simply short-term noise you can ignore.
